## Post 2 – Thu Oct 15, 10:00 am – The 90-day business plan

Before you buy a single feeder animal, your fattening plan should answer four numbers:

1️⃣ Total capital required (purchase + 90 days of costs + contingency)
2️⃣ Cost per kg gained
3️⃣ Break-even selling price per kg
4️⃣ Expected profit per animal bought

Illustrative example: 20 bachra (young bulls) bought at 180 kg for PKR 160,000 each, fed at PKR 450/day for 90 days.

• Total investment: PKR 4.3 million
• Break-even: about PKR 898 per kg live
• Profit at PKR 1,000/kg: about PKR 488,000, or PKR 24,400 per animal

And the part most plans skip: capital. PKR 3.2 million goes out on day one, and another PKR 1.1 million before the first sale. Add about 10% contingency.

Then stress-test it. If daily gain drops from 0.8 to 0.6 kg, profit falls to about PKR 146,000.

## Post 3 – Fri Oct 16, 10:00 am – Is it the feed or the animal?

"My cattle are gaining slowly. Is it the feed or the animals I bought?"

It's one of the most common questions in fattening, and you can't answer it without records.

What a two-week weighing routine tells you:
• The whole pen is slow → look at the ration, water and step-up feeding
• A few animals are slow → look at health, age or the source they came from
• One supplier's animals are always behind → change how or where you buy

Why it matters (illustrative example, 10 head, 120 days):
At 0.9 kg/day: profit about USD 1,617, cost per kg gained USD 3.88
At 0.6 kg/day: profit about USD 488, cost per kg gained USD 5.82

Same animals, same feed bill. The difference is daily gain, and you only see it if you weigh.

No scale? A calibrated weigh band on a fixed sample of animals is enough to spot a problem early.

## Post 4 – Mon Oct 19, 10:00 am – Know your break-even before you buy

The most profitable decision in cattle fattening happens at the market, before any feed is bought.

The feeder animal is usually the single biggest cost. A Canfax feedlot cost study for Alberta Beef Producers found it made up 60–75% of non-factor costs in Australia, the US and Canada.

So before buying, work out the highest price per kg you can pay and still make money:

1. Expected sale weight × expected sale price = sale value per head
2. Subtract feed, health, labour and other costs per head for the cycle
3. What's left is the most you can pay for the animal, before any profit

For Eid al-Adha 2027 (expected mid-May, depending on moon sighting), a 90-day cycle means buying by early February. That makes now the time to set your maximum purchase price.

Running 300 to 1,000 head? You'll eventually face this choice: build custom feedlot software, or use a ready-made system.

Custom build
✅ Fits every unique process
❌ Months of development before day one
❌ High upfront cost, plus ongoing maintenance

Ready-made system
✅ Live in days or weeks

### New feedlot, new disease risk

A new feedlot brings animals from many sources into one place, and that's exactly when disease risk is highest.

Kenyan farmers have described close calls with lumpy skin disease (LSD) in newly stocked feedlots. Contagious bovine pleuropneumonia (CBPP) is another concern in parts of East Africa.

Good practice before new cattle join the main pens:
• Isolation or quarantine for about two weeks
• Vaccination and deworming on a programme agreed with your veterinarian
• A health record for every animal: what it was treated with, when, and its withdrawal date

These records also matter more each year. Kenya (ANITRAC) and Ethiopia (ETLITS) are rolling out digital livestock identification and traceability.

Per-animal health records protect the herd, the buyer and the business.
